Prompt engineering refers to the practice of carefully designing the input
text given to a language model in order to get more accurate, relevant, or
consistently formatted outputs, without changing the model itself. In a RAG
system, the prompt is where the retrieved context gets combined with the
user's question before being sent to the model, so the way that prompt is
structured has a significant effect on answer quality.

A common and effective technique is to explicitly instruct the model to only
answer using the provided context, and to say it does not know the answer if
the context does not contain it. Without this instruction, language models
will often fall back on their own internal training knowledge to fill gaps,
which defeats the purpose of retrieval grounding and can reintroduce
hallucination, especially for questions the retrieved chunks only partially
answer.

Another useful technique is clearly labeling each piece of retrieved context
with its source, for example including the filename or document title above
each chunk in the prompt. This allows the model to cite where information
came from in its answer, which makes the system's outputs easier to verify
and builds user trust, particularly important in professional or research
settings where unverified claims are risky.

The order in which context chunks are presented in the prompt can also
matter. Some research has found that language models pay more attention to
information placed at the very beginning or the very end of a long prompt,
and can partially ignore information buried in the middle, an effect
sometimes called "lost in the middle." This is one reason reranking matters,
since it ensures the most relevant chunks are not just included, but placed
prominently.
